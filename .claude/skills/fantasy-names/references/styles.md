# The Ten Styles

Each entry gives: what the style *is*, the phonetic signature, the build
formula, word-banks, worked examples, and what it's for. Read the entry for the
style you're using before you write a name — the rules are what keep output from
collapsing into generic fantasy mush.

## 1. Anglo-Gothic

**Exemplars:** Bartholomew Grimsditch · Beric Dondarrion (partly) · Ravensway

**Definition.** A plain, heavy, *real* archaic English given name paired with an
invented-but-plausible English surname assembled from concrete, grim nouns. The
whole effect comes from the collision: a name that could belong to a parish
clerk or a tax assessor, attached to someone who eats minds.

**Why it works.** Real English surnames are toponymic — they're places. Grim's
ditch was an actual earthwork in Cheshire before it was ever a person. So a name
built this way reads as *inherited history* rather than invention, which grounds
a weird character in a world that had a land registry. That grounding is the
whole point: the horror feels like it came with the property.

**Phonetics.** Short vowels, hard consonants, a stressed first syllable in the
surname. Two syllables in the surname, occasionally three. No liquids-only
names; you want at least one stop (k, t, d, b, g).

**Formula.** `[Archaic English given name] + [modifier] + [landform or structure]`

*Given names* (use real ones — the ordinariness is load-bearing):
Bartholomew, Ambrose, Cuthbert, Ezekiel, Horatio, Obadiah, Percival, Alaric,
Mordecai, Thaddeus, Barnaby, Silas, Josiah, Hezekiah, Tobias, Wilfred, Erasmus,
Cornelius, Ignatius, Aldous, Grenville, Peregrine, Bertram, Osric.
Feminine: Wilhelmina, Prudence, Temperance, Millicent, Hester, Agatha, Dorcas,
Rosamund, Cordelia, Winifred, Bathsheba, Eugenia, Maud, Clemency.

*Surname modifiers:* grim, ash, crow, thorn, gallow, nettle, sorrow, wain,
black, bell, cold, mourn, quill, hollow, rook, brack, sallow, pike, winter, dun.

*Surname landforms (the second half):* -ditch, -barrow, -moor, -hollow,
-thwaite, -combe, -stoke, -worth, -wick, -marsh, -fen, -mire, -reach, -hallow,
-church, -yard, -crook, -bourne, -shaw, -holt, -mere, -garth, -cote, -stead.

**Worked examples.**
- *Grimsditch* — grim + ditch. Note the possessive **-s-**: "Grim's ditch."
  That linking *s* is the single most authenticating trick in this style.
- *Crowthwaite* — crow + thwaite (a Norse clearing). Names a place no one visits.
- *Gallowfen* — gallow + fen. Where they hanged people in a bog.
- *Sallowmere* — sallow + mere. A sickly lake; also the person's complexion.
- *Quillstoke* — quill + stoke. A scribe's hamlet. Good for a scholar.

**Craft notes.** Drop a linking *-s-* or *-en-* between halves when the joint is
clumsy (Grim**s**ditch, Ash**en**marsh). Avoid surnames that are already loaded
in English — Blackwood and Ravenscroft are fine but tired; go one degree
stranger. The best of these sound like somewhere you could look up on a map.

**Use for.** Wizards, scholars, eldritch-touched, anything Victorian-gothic,
family curses, and any character whose strangeness should feel *inherited*
rather than chosen.

## 2. High Sindarin

**Exemplars:** Húrin Thalion · Seshen Thalion · Luztherin Theramon · Galadriel ·
Lews Therin Telamon · Mandragoran

**Definition.** A liquid-flowing given name followed by a second name that isn't
a family name at all — it's a *meaning*, an epithet the world granted and now
uses as if it were a surname. *Thalion* means "the Steadfast." Húrin didn't
inherit it; he earned it and it stuck.

**Why it works.** It implies a language, and a language implies a civilization
with several thousand years of history behind this one person. The reader never
learns the language, and doesn't need to — the *consistency* of the sounds does
the work.

**Phonetics.** The spine is **l, r, th, n, m, s**. Stops (k, g, t, p, b) appear
sparingly and never in clusters. Vowels stay open and pure: a, e, i, o — avoid
diphthongs like *ea*, *ou*, *ai* except at the very start. Two to four
syllables, stress on the first or second.

*Endings that carry the register:* -ion, -iel, -on, -in, -or, -eth, -as, -ir,
-il, -ath, -uin, -ael, -amon, -ain.

**Formula.** `[Root] + [Root] + [ending]`, where at least one root means
something about the character.

*Root bank (Sindarin-flavored, meanings usable as-is):*
thal- steadfast · gal-/galad- light, radiance · mor- dark · cel- swift ·
ael- lake · luin- blue · nim- white · ross- dew, foam · tir- watch, guard ·
dûn- west · lin- song · eär- sea · cur- skill, craft · bel- strong ·
fir- mortal, fading · nár- fire · orod- mountain · gwaith- people ·
rond- vault · thar- across, beyond · maeg- sharp · heru- lord.

**Worked examples.**
- *Thalion* = thal (steadfast) + -ion. The archetype.
- *Theramon* (author's) = ther- + -amon, patterned on Telamon; reads as
  "pillar/bearer," which suits a martial artist chosen by a god.
- *Morlindel* = mor (dark) + lin (song) + -del. A singer of dark things.
- *Cel**e**thir* = cel (swift) + tir (watch); the inserted *e* keeps it flowing.
- *Narosseth* = nár (fire) + ross (foam) + -eth. Fire on water.

**The Jordan variant.** Robert Jordan's Age of Legends register (Lews Therin
Telamon, Mandragoran) is the same phonetics stretched longer and pulled toward
Greek and Latin in the *final* element: -amon, -mon, -on, -oran, -agoran. Split
the given name into two words (Lews Therin, not Lewstherin) and the name
instantly sounds three thousand years old. Mandragoran = mandragora (mandrake,
the screaming root) + -an. Use this variant for ancient, tragic, or fallen
figures.

**Craft notes.** Never let an epithet-surname be *nice*. Thalion, Telamon,
Mandragoran, Theramon all have weight and a bit of iron in them. And the
character should genuinely *be* the thing the epithet says — that's why it
sticks.

**Use for.** Elves, ancient bloodlines, tragic heroes, high-register sorcerers,
oathbound knights, anything where the character's reputation is older than the
campaign.

## 3. Kenning-Byname

**Exemplars:** Origajima **Foldspire** · Dondarrion **Ravensway** · Kaladin
**Stormblessed** · Logen **the Bloody Nine** · **Lord of the Morning**

**Definition.** The second name is a plain-English compound that *states the
power or the deed outright*. Nothing hidden. Foldspire says: he folds paper, he
builds towers out of it. Stormblessed says: something happened to him in a storm
and it wasn't a curse.

**Why it works.** After the buried-meaning styles, this one is a relief — it's
the name the *common people* use, because they don't speak Sindarin and don't
know what *morde* means. Pairing a Kenning-Byname with an exotic given name
gives you both layers at once: the name his mother gave him, and the name the
soldiers gave him. That's why it layers onto everything.

**Three formulas.**

*A — the compound byname.* `[Power noun] + [structure / motion / state noun]`
- Power halves: fold, storm, ash, grave, dawn, iron, salt, thorn, ember, wake,
  hush, tide, bone, frost, gild, rust, hollow, vow.
- Second halves: -spire, -way, -blessed, -march, -breaker, -walker, -bearer,
  -fall, -wright, -reach, -kept, -shorn, -bound, -tender, -crowned, -sung.
- Examples: Foldspire · Ravensway · Ashenmarch · Gravewalker · Saltcrowned ·
  Hushbearer · Vowshorn · Emberkept.

*B — the definite byname.* `[Name], the [Adjective] [Noun]`
- Balerion, the Black Dread · Logen, the Bloody Nine.
- The noun should be slightly *wrong* for a person: a Dread, a Nine, a Quiet,
  a Verdict. Never "the Mighty Warrior."

*C — the courtly title.* `[Object / Rank] of the [Abstract or time]`
- Lord of the Morning · Sword of the Evening · Keeper of the Last Hour.
- See `references/epithets.md` for much more on this one.

**The craft rule that matters.** The compound must be *slightly strange*.
"Stormbringer" is a sword you've heard of; "Stormblessed" is a person. The trick
is to pick the less obvious half of the pair:
- not Foldmaster but **Foldspire**
- not Ravenlord but **Ravensway**
- not Deathwalker but **Gravewalker**, or better, **Gravetender**
- not Stormbringer but **Stormblessed**

When in doubt, swap the second half for something *gentler* than the first. The
mismatch is what makes it land.

**Use for.** Anyone whose reputation precedes them. Works best as a *second*
layer on top of another style's given name — which is exactly what the corpus
does (Origajima + Foldspire, Dondarrion + Ravensway).

## 4. Concept-Loan

**Exemplars:** Yuddha Rama Samsara · Ankara Citra · Seshen · Merveya

**Definition.** Every element is a real word from a real language, chosen
because it *means* the character. The name isn't decorated with meaning — it
*is* the meaning. Yuddha = war. Rama = the exiled warrior-king. Samsara = the
cycle of death and rebirth. Three words, and you have a man who was war, who
walked away like Rama walked into exile, and who is trying to get off the wheel.
He's a fisherman now. The name already told you.

**Why it works.** Real words carry real phonotactics — they sound like they
came from a mouth, not a keyboard. And the meanings are *true*, so the name
rewards a reader who looks it up without punishing one who doesn't.

**Method.**
1. Write the character's thesis in one sentence.
2. Pull 2–3 nouns out of it.
3. Translate each into a language whose *sound* fits the character's culture.
4. Order them so the name scans: usually short-short-long, or long-short-long.
5. Check nothing accidentally means something absurd or offensive; adjust a
   vowel if it does.

**Word banks** (full inventories in `references/phonetics.md`):

*Sanskrit / Pali* — yuddha (war), shanti (peace), agni (fire), vayu (wind),
kala (time, death), maya (illusion), tejas (radiance), vajra (thunderbolt,
diamond), ananta (endless), mrityu (death), atman (self), dharma, karuna
(compassion), virya (valor), shakti (power), nira (water), asura, deva, samsara,
moksha (release), avidya (ignorance), rudra (howler), soma.

*Egyptian* — seshen (lotus), akhet (horizon), sekhem (power), duat (underworld),
maat (order), ren (true name), ka, ba, neter (divine), heka (magic), khepri
(becoming), nebu (gold), wadjet, shemsu (follower).

*Persian / Turkic* — darya (sea), azar (fire), mehr (sun, love), shahin
(falcon), yildiz (star), kaya (rock), sipahi (cavalry), anahita, bahram,
zarrin (golden), simurgh, kerman, alp (hero), tugra (seal).

*Romance / Latin* — merveille (marvel → Merveya), lux/luz (light), mordere
(to bite), fides (faith), umbra (shade), vesper (evening), nox, ferrum (iron),
sanguis (blood), gelu (frost), avaritia, oblivium.

**Craft rule.** *Don't translate the concept into English inside the same name.*
"Yuddha Warborn" collapses it. Let the meaning stay buried — that's what
separates this from Kenning-Byname, which does the opposite on purpose.

**Use for.** Monks, swordmasters, druids, clerics, philosophers, and any
character from a culture outside the pseudo-European default. Especially good
for characters whose *name is an argument about how to live*.

## 5. Portmanteau-Root

**Exemplars:** Mordekaisa Levisay · Balaurung · Origajima · Luztherin · Merveya

**Definition.** Weld two meaning-carrying roots from two different languages
into a single word that scans as one name. *Morde* (Latin *mordere*, to bite;
Spanish *muerte*) + *kaisa* (kaisar, emperor) = a woman who bit the throat out
of an empire. *Balaur* (Romanian: dragon) + *-ung* (Germanic: scion of) = a man
with the blood of a dragon, and it also happens to rhyme with Glaurung, which
does half the work for free.

**Why it works.** This is the author's signature move and the highest-leverage
style here. It produces names no one else has, that mean exactly what you want,
and that sound like a real language you've never studied.

**The five-step weld.**
1. **Sentence.** "A general who murdered her own empire to protect her family."
2. **Two nouns.** murder + emperor.
3. **Two languages.** Latin *mordere* → *morde*. German *Kaiser* → *kaisa(r)*.
   Pick languages whose phonetics you like, not whose meanings are closest.
4. **Overlap and fuse.** Find a shared sound and slide the words into each other:
   `morde` + `kaisar` → `morde|kaisa` → **Mordekaisa**. The trailing *r* gets
   dropped because it stops the flow.
5. **Sand the seam.** Say it. Adjust vowels until it reads as one word, not two
   glued. If you can hear the join, change a vowel at the joint — that's almost
   always the fix.

*Joint and ending kit:* -ung, -in, -a, -is, -oth, -ar, -eya, -aine, -ova,
-esh, -im, -ai, -un, -or, -ka, -ja, -ren, -thal.

**Worked examples from the corpus.**
- *Origajima* = origami + -jima (Japanese island suffix, as in Iwojima). Note
  it does **not** say "paper." It says "origami-island" — a place, not a power.
- *Luztherin* = luz (Spanish, light) + -therin (lifted from Lews Therin). A
  fistfighter chosen by a deity: light, in a Jordan-register frame.
- *Merveya* = merveille (French, marvel) + -eya. A bodyguard who did something
  marvelous, i.e. won.
- *Balaurung* = balaur (Romanian dragon) + -ung (Germanic "descendant of").

**Craft notes.** Two languages, never three — three roots produce soup. If the
result is unpronounceable, the problem is nearly always a consonant cluster at
the seam; insert a vowel there. And resist the temptation to pick roots that
*already* sound fantasy; ordinary words from ordinary languages weld better.

**Use for.** Villains, dragon-blooded, sorcerers, anyone whose *concept* should
be encoded rather than announced. Also the best style for a mononym.

## 6. Apostrophe-Clan

**Exemplars:** Merveya **Ashe'kani** · Xochimeh **Tepoch'onnya**

**Definition.** A name whose apostrophe marks a real *joint* — a clan prefix, a
lineage particle, or a glottal stop in the language. Not decoration.

**Why most fantasy gets this wrong, and how to not.** An apostrophe dropped in
at random is the loudest possible signal that a name was made up in thirty
seconds. The fix is one decision: **decide what the apostrophe means in your
world, and be consistent.** Ashe'kani reads as *of the Ashe line, kani sept*.
Tepoch'onnya splits at a glottal stop the way real Nahuatl and Mayan words do.
Once the mark has a job, the name has a culture behind it.

**Rules.**
- **One apostrophe per name. Never two.**
- It sits between two independently pronounceable halves.
- Never at the start, never at the end, never beside another apostrophe.
- Removing it should change the *pronunciation*, not destroy it. If
  `Ashekani` is unpronounceable, you used the mark as decoration.
- Don't give apostrophes to every member of a culture *and* their neighbours.
  Scarcity is what makes it read as a specific lineage marker.

**Formula.** `[Clan or lineage root] ' [Personal or sept element]`

*Lineage roots:* ashe-, kae-, zul-, vash-, dre-, ith-, sul-, kha-, orr-, tsae-,
ny-, vor-, sha-, eze-.
*Sept elements:* -kani, -meth, -thi, -ra, -jin, -vek, -aan, -oru, -isk, -endi,
-ashi, -uun, -oth.

**The Mesoamerican variant** (Xochimeh Tepoch'onnya). A distinct sub-register
with its own phonetics: **x-, tz-, -tl, -tli, -meh** (plural), **-tzin**
(honorific). The apostrophe is a glottal stop and appears mid-word.

*Roots:* xochi (flower), coatl (serpent), itz (obsidian), teo (divine),
mictl (death), quetzal, ocelo (jaguar), tepoch (youth), tlalli (earth),
ehecatl (wind), citlal (star), atl (water), tonal (day, soul), nahual (shapechanger).

*Examples:* Xochimeh · Tepoch'onnya · Itzcoa'tlan · Ocelomeh Citlal'nahua ·
Mictla'tzin. For a druid who becomes hundreds of animals, *nahual* is right
there and means exactly that.

**Use for.** Tribal cultures, desert and drow clans, Mesoamerican-flavored
peoples, and anywhere you want a culture that visibly isn't European and has its
own grammar rather than just its own costume.

## 7. Sentence-Name

**Exemplar:** Stranger-Comes-Knocking

**Definition.** A full clause, hyphenated and capitalized, used as a name. It
does not describe the person — it describes **the moment they became
themselves**, or what happens when they arrive.

**Why it works.** It implies a culture that names people for *events* rather
than lineage, which tells you as much about the world as the character. And it's
unsettling in exactly the right way for outsiders and inquisitors, because
you're not being told who someone is — you're being told what they do to a room.

**Formula.** `[Subject] - [Present-tense verb] - [Complement]`, three to four
words, hyphenated, every word capitalized.

- **Subject:** an archetype or an object, never a proper name.
  Stranger, Crow, Door, Ash, Winter, Lantern, Salt, Wolf, Mother, Rope, Question.
- **Verb:** present tense, physical, and either ominous or oddly gentle.
  Comes, Waits, Opens, Counts, Remembers, Sings, Walks, Knocks, Listens, Forgets,
  Never-Sleeps, Does-Not-Turn.
- **Complement:** a place, a state, or a direction. Knocking, In-Salt, Inward,
  The-Sleeping, At-The-Ford, Twice.

**Good:**
Stranger-Comes-Knocking · Wolf-Counts-The-Sleeping · Door-That-Opens-Inward ·
She-Waits-In-Salt · Lantern-Left-Burning · Ash-Falls-Upward ·
Mother-Asks-Again · Crow-Knows-Your-Road.

**Bad, and why:**
- *Mighty-Warrior-Strong* — adjective stack, no event, nothing happens.
- *Killer-Of-Many-Men* — a stat block, not a moment.
- *He-Who-Walks-In-Darkness* — generic; "darkness" is an abstraction and the
  clause could describe anyone.

**The test.** The name should imply a *scene*. If someone could paint it, it
works. If they'd have to paint a résumé, it doesn't.

**Use for.** Spirits, inquisitors, fey, outsiders, eldritch-bound characters,
and cultures that name for deeds. Also excellent as a *true name* hidden behind
an ordinary alias.

## 8. Imperial Rolling

**Exemplar:** King Damarion Chimamandara

**Definition.** Long, vowel-rich, open-syllabled names built to be **announced**.
Every syllable is a consonant-vowel pair, so the name pours out rather than
clicking: Chi-ma-man-da-ra. Da-ma-ri-on.

**Why it works.** Say "Grimsditch" and then say "Chimamandara." The first stops
three times; the second never stops at all. A name that can't be interrupted
sounds like a name that has been said in throne rooms for four hundred years —
which is precisely the effect you want for a dynasty.

**Phonetics.**
- Open vowels: **a, o, i**. Very little e or u.
- Consonant spine: **m, n, r, d, l, ch, s**. No clusters *at all* — no *str*,
  *nd*, *rk*. If you catch a cluster, insert a vowel.
- Four to six syllables in the surname; three to four in the given name.
- **Reduplicate a syllable somewhere.** Chi-ma-**man**-da-ra repeats the *ma*.
  That echo is what makes it feel ancestral rather than merely long.

**Formula.** `[Given: 3-4 open syllables, ending -ion / -ius / -aro / -ande] +
[Surname: 4-6 open syllables with an internal echo]`

*Given-name kit:* Dama-, Kesa-, Olu-, Ade-, Tewo-, Mena-, Zala-, Ifea-, Bara-,
Nsia-, Amara-, Oyo-, Taresa-. Endings: -rion, -ion, -ius, -aro, -ande, -ike,
-eze, -mide.

*Surname kit:* build from CV syllables — chi, ma, da, ra, na, la, ne, to, sa,
be, ro, ka, mi, yo, wa, du, si, ba. Then repeat one of them.

**Worked examples.**
- *Chimamandara* (author's) — Chimamanda (Igbo: "my spirit will not be broken")
  + -ra. The meaning is perfect for an eighty-year-old paladin still on the
  field, which is why it works and not just because it's pretty.
- *Adesolanaro* — ade (crown) + sola + naro; the *a* recurs four times.
- *Oluwaremide* — olu + wa + re + mi + de.
- *Kesarionde* — kesa + ri + onde.

**Sources to raid.** Igbo and Yoruba (Chimamanda, Adaeze, Olumide, Oluwaseun),
Swahili, Ethiopian/Amharic (Tewodros, Menelik, Zewditu), Malagasy, Sanskrit's
longer compounds.

**Use for.** Kings, emperors, high paladins, archmages, dynastic houses, and
anyone whose surname should sound like the last four hundred years of a family.
Pairs beautifully with a short blunt title: *King Damarion Chimamandara*.

## 9. Compact Duotone

**Exemplars:** Norra Kaida · Ankara Citra · Seshen Thalion

**Definition.** Two short names, two syllables each, drawn from two *different*
phonetic families. The whole name lands in under a second. Norra (Nordic —
*norr*, north; cold, clean) + Kaida (Japanese — "little dragon"; sharp, bright).
Two cultures colliding inside one person.

**Why it works.** This is the style for a name you will say out loud three
hundred times. Long names get abbreviated at the table; a Compact Duotone
*survives intact*, which means the whole name keeps doing its job all campaign.
And the contrast between halves does the characterization that length would
otherwise have to.

**Rules.**
- **2 + 2 syllables.** Occasionally 2 + 3, never 3 + 3.
- The halves must come from **different phonetic families**. One soft, one hard.
  One Nordic, one Japanese. One Latinate, one Turkic. The seam is the point.
- Matching final vowels twin the halves and make it melodic (Norr**a**
  Kaid**a**, Anka**ra** Cit**ra**). Contrasting them makes it snap
  (Sesh**en** Thali**on**).
- Both halves should be *real or near-real words*. Norra, Kaida, Ankara, and
  Citra all exist.

**Worked examples.**
- *Norra Kaida* — Norse cold + Japanese dragon. For a sorceress of blood and
  fluids: the name is clean and small, and everything about her isn't.
- *Ankara Citra* — Turkish city + Sanskrit *citra* (variegated, many-hued). A
  nature magician; "many-hued" is the whole class feature.
- *Vasha Kirin* — Slavic + Japanese.
- *Runa Teshka* — Norse rune + Slavic diminutive.
- *Suri Valdan* — Quechua + Germanic.

**Use for.** Player characters, rogues, sorcerers, anyone modern-feeling and
fast, and any name that has to work in a shout across a battlefield.

## 10. Harsh Particle

**Exemplar:** Sand dan Glokta

**Definition.** A deliberately **ugly** name with an aristocratic particle
stranded in the middle. The particle says nobility; the phonetics say the
nobility rotted. Sand dan Glokta was a fencing champion; then two years in an
Emperor's prison made him a torturer with a ruined body. The name has both men
in it — *dan* is the fencing champion, *Glokta* is what came back.

**Why it works.** Every other style here is trying to sound *good*. This one
is trying to sound like a man clearing his throat. In a grimdark world that
contrast is the entire register, and it's the fastest way to signal that the
aristocracy in your setting is not to be admired.

**Phonetics.** Hard clusters and dead stops: **gl, kt, br, zh, sk, pf, rst,
tch, kr, sn**. Short. Clipped. **No liquid flow, no open final vowels.** End on
a consonant or a flat *-a*.

*Ugly surname bank:* Glokta, Vetch, Krast, Burr, Sult, Pike, Nubb, Skarn,
Gorst, Brack, Tellik, Vosk, Hurd, Grist, Kroy, Mauthe, Sneck, Drubb, Talk,
Rache, Pfaltz, Stang.
*Given names — short and plain:* Sand, Jez, Caul, Bayaz, Nic, Fenn, Ruth, Corm,
Vell, Hale, Ost, Bryn, Marsh.
*Particles:* dan, ven, ap, el, ter, zo, na, vor, dis.

**Formula.** `[Short plain given] + [particle] + [ugly surname]`
- Sand dan Glokta · Corm ven Skarn · Jez ap Brack · Vell ter Gorst ·
  Hale zo Drubb · Fenn el Vosk.

**Craft rule.** This style dies the moment the name becomes pretty. Read it
aloud: **if it sounds like it belongs on a sword, start over.** It should sound
like it belongs on a ledger, or a warrant, or a cell door.

**Use for.** Grimdark settings, torturers, spymasters, inquisitors, fallen
aristocracy, bureaucrats with power — anyone in a world where a noble title is
a stain rather than an honour.

## The stacking dial

Independent of style: **how many parts does the name have?** This is pure
register control and it's the cheapest way to change a name's weight.

| Parts | Effect | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Elemental, mythic, or inhuman. No family, no context. | Balaurung, Galadriel, Mandragoran |
| **2** | The default. A person you can talk to. | Norra Kaida, Seshen Thalion, Origajima Foldspire |
| **3** | Legendary, ancient, or ceremonial. Reads as *history*. | Lews Therin Telamon, Yuddha Rama Samsara, Priest Dondarrion Ravensway |
| **4+** | Aristocratic, absurd, or both — a name that is also a claim. | Samantha Princesza Jazzyka Engka |

Notes on the longer stacks:

- **Three parts** is the sweet spot for a figure out of legend. It works because
  the middle element reads as something the reader can't quite place — a house,
  a lineage, a discipline. Yuddha *Rama* Samsara: the middle name is a god's.
- **Four parts** can hold a **title used as a name** — *Princesza* in Samantha
  Princesza Jazzyka Engka is "princess" wearing a Polish suffix, sitting where a
  middle name should be. It's a name that is making an assertion about status,
  which is characterization by itself. Use it for someone who has *claimed*
  something rather than inherited it.
- A mononym plus a byname (Balerion, the Black Dread) counts as one part plus an
  epithet, not two parts — see `references/epithets.md`.

## Choosing a style

Start from the character, not from the list.

| If the character is... | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Cursed by inheritance, scholarly, gothic | Anglo-Gothic |
| Ancient, tragic, high-born, oathbound | High Sindarin |
| Known by reputation; the commons have a name for them | Kenning-Byname (layered) |
| Defined by a philosophy or a discipline | Concept-Loan |
| Built around one strong concept; a villain; a mononym | Portmanteau-Root |
| From a specific non-European culture with its own grammar | Apostrophe-Clan |
| An outsider, spirit, or inquisitor | Sentence-Name |
| Royal, dynastic, or announced before they enter | Imperial Rolling |
| A PC you'll say out loud all campaign | Compact Duotone |
| Grimdark, bureaucratic, rotted nobility | Harsh Particle |

Then **layer a second style** onto the other half of the name (see the layering
principle in SKILL.md) — that's where most of the quality lives.
